Pineapple plant (Ananas comosus) care

Bromeliads & air plants · straightforward to keep

Last updated 15 October 2026

Pineapple plant (Ananas comosus) wants full sun — the brightest window you have. Water it weekly. Water the compost when the top few centimetres are dry. It stores water in its leaves and tolerates drying out. Pot it in gritty, free-draining compost. A very weak feed occasionally, into the central cup rather than the compost, and repot every 2 years. The pineapple: a rosette of stiff, spiny-edged leaves that, after two or three years and plenty of sun, sends up a single small pineapple. Many are grown from the top of a shop-bought fruit.

Watering a Pineapple plant

Water weekly. Water the compost when the top few centimetres are dry. It stores water in its leaves and tolerates drying out.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Bright indirect light. Bromeliads take more shade than most flowering plants but need brightness to keep their colour.

Humidity and temperature

Room humidity is fine. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

Bromeliads are epiphytes with token root systems used mostly for anchorage. Orchid bark with a little compost, in a small pot, is right.

Feeding

A very weak feed occasionally, into the central cup rather than the compost. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot every 2 years. Rarely. The parent rosette flowers once and then slowly dies over a year or two, leaving pups around its base — those are what you pot on.

Common problems

  • Leaves browning from the tips

    Too little light, or dry, stuffy air.

    Fix: Move it into full sun; it is a field crop, not a forest plant.

    Brown tips and edges: how to read it on any plant

  • Rot at the base

    The compost stayed wet.

    Fix: Let it dry between waterings and use a gritty mix.

  • No fruit

    It takes two or three years, and plenty of light.

    Fix: Patience, and the sunniest spot you have. Each plant fruits once and then makes pups.

Propagation

Twist the leafy top off a shop pineapple, strip the lowest leaves, let it dry for a few days, then root it in water or gritty compost.

A cutting taken in PlantParlour keeps its link to the plant it came from, so a propagation years later still traces back to the mother plant — which is the point if you ever sell or trade it.

Toxicity and safety

Not on the ASPCA's list of toxic or non-toxic plants, so we don't call it pet-safe: not being listed is not the same as being tested. Keep it out of reach of a pet that chews.

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. We don't test plants: what we say about toxicity follows the ASPCA's published plant list as it stood on 15 October 2026, and that list can change. "Non-toxic" means not poisonous — a pet that eats enough of any plant can still vomit, and fertiliser, pesticide or leaf shine on a plant can harm it. It also only helps if the plant is what you think it is. It covers cats and dogs only; ask your vet about rabbits, birds, reptiles or anything else, and a doctor about children. Read our terms.
