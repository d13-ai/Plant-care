Hoya linearis care

The odd one out among hoyas: soft, fuzzy, needle-thin leaves hanging in long green curtains, more like a string plant than a wax plant. It comes from cool Himalayan forests, so it wants better humidity, more air movement and cooler nights than the leathery hoyas — and it rots faster if kept wet.

Watering a Hoya linearis

Water every 8 days. Water thoroughly when the pot is nearly dry and the leaves have just started to feel less firm. Hoyas store water in their leaves and are far more tolerant of drought than of wet feet.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Bright indirect light, and a couple of hours of direct morning sun if you want flowers. Hoyas grown in medium light stay alive and green for years without ever blooming.

Humidity and temperature

Above 60%, with air moving. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

Hoyas are epiphytes that root into bark and moss. A chunky mix of bark and perlite with a little compost is right; ordinary potting soil holds far too much water.

Feeding

A balanced feed at half strength every second or third watering in spring and summer. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot every 2 years. Rarely. Hoyas flower best tightly potbound, and repotting a plant that is about to bloom usually costs you the flowers. Every three or four years is plenty.

Common problems

  • Never flowers

    Not enough light, too much pot, or the flower spurs were cut off.

    Fix: Move it brighter, stop repotting, and never cut the old flower stalks — hoyas bloom from the same short spurs year after year.

  • Wrinkled, dimpled leaves

    Under-watering — or, confusingly, root rot, which stops the plant drinking.

    Fix: Check the roots. Firm white roots mean water it; brown mushy roots mean repot and cut the damage away.

  • Yellow leaves dropping

    Overwatering or cold.

    Fix: Let it dry out further and keep it above 15 °C.

    Yellow leaves: how to read it on any plant

  • Sticky black film under the flowers

    The flowers themselves drip nectar; it is not a pest.

    Fix: Wipe it off. Put a saucer under a plant that is blooming heavily.

Propagation

Cuttings with two nodes and a leaf or two root in water, perlite or sphagnum over four to eight weeks.

A cutting taken in PlantParlour keeps its link to the plant it came from, so a propagation years later still traces back to the mother plant — which is the point if you ever sell or trade it.

Toxicity and safety

Not on the ASPCA's list itself, only its relatives, so we don't call it pet-safe. Keep it out of reach of a pet that chews.

The ASPCA does not list Hoya linearis itself, but lists a relative, Hindu Rope Plant (Hoya carnosa 'krinkle kurl'), as non-toxic to cats and dogs (checked 24 September 2026).

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. We don't test plants: what we say about toxicity follows the ASPCA's published plant list as it stood on 24 September 2026, and that list can change. "Non-toxic" means not poisonous — a pet that eats enough of any plant can still vomit, and fertiliser, pesticide or leaf shine on a plant can harm it. It also only helps if the plant is what you think it is. It covers cats and dogs only; ask your vet about rabbits, birds, reptiles or anything else, and a doctor about children. Read our terms.
