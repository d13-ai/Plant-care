Chinese hibiscus (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis) care

Flowering · needs attention to keep

Chinese hibiscus (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis) wants full sun. Water it every 4 days. Keep damp through the growing season — daily in a hot room is normal — and much drier in winter. Pot it in rich loam-based compost, free-draining. A high-potash feed weekly through spring and summer, and repot once a year. Humidity: above 50%. Not on the ASPCA's list itself, only its relatives, so we don't call it pet-safe.

Chinese hibiscus: enormous flowers, each lasting a day or two, produced all summer on new growth if it gets enough sun and enough feeding. It is greedy on both counts and will not flower indoors without a genuinely sunny window.

Watering a Chinese hibiscus

Water every 4 days. Keep damp through the growing season — daily in a hot room is normal — and much drier in winter.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

As much direct sun as possible — six hours if you can. Hibiscus flower on new growth in strong light and simply do not bloom without it.

Humidity and temperature

Above 50%. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

A rich loam-based compost with grit. They are hungry and thirsty and still want the pot to drain.

Feeding

A high-potash feed weekly through spring and summer. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot once a year. Every spring while young. Prune in early spring to shape — flowers come on new wood.

Common problems

  • Buds dropping before they open

    Irregular watering, a move, or a sudden temperature change.

    Fix: Water on a steady routine and keep it in one place.

  • Yellow leaves

    Overwatering, cold, or hunger.

    Fix: Check the drainage first, then feed — hibiscus are greedy.

    Yellow leaves: how to read it on any plant

  • No flowers

    Not enough light or not enough potash.

    Fix: Move it into full sun and switch to a high-potash feed.

Propagation

Softwood cuttings in early summer with bottom heat.

A cutting taken in PlantParlour keeps its link to the plant it came from, so a propagation years later still traces back to the mother plant — which is the point if you ever sell or trade it.

Toxicity and safety

Not on the ASPCA's list itself, only its relatives, so we don't call it pet-safe. Keep it out of reach of a pet that chews.

The ASPCA does not list Hibiscus rosa-sinensis itself, but lists a relative, Hibiscus (Hibiscus syriacus), as non-toxic to cats and dogs (checked 24 September 2026).

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. We don't test plants: what we say about toxicity follows the ASPCA's published plant list as it stood on 24 September 2026, and that list can change. "Non-toxic" means not poisonous — a pet that eats enough of any plant can still vomit, and fertiliser, pesticide or leaf shine on a plant can harm it. It also only helps if the plant is what you think it is. It covers cats and dogs only; ask your vet about rabbits, birds, reptiles or anything else, and a doctor about children. Read our terms.
