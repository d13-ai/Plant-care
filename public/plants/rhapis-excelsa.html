Lady palm (Rhapis excelsa) care

Palms · easy to keep

Lady palm (Rhapis excelsa) wants medium indirect light. Water it weekly. Keep the top of the compost just barely damp. Palms will not forgive a pot left standing in water, and brown tips are the receipt for the one time you let it dry out hard. Pot it in standard compost with perlite. A weak balanced feed monthly in spring and summer only, and repot every 2 years. Humidity: above 50% keeps the tips green. The ASPCA lists it as non-toxic to cats and dogs.

The lady palm: fan-shaped fronds of blunt-ended segments on slender bamboo-like canes, forming a dense clump. Unusually for a palm it suckers, so it divides, and it copes with low light and dry air better than most.

Watering a Lady palm

Water weekly. Keep the top of the compost just barely damp. Palms will not forgive a pot left standing in water, and brown tips are the receipt for the one time you let it dry out hard.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Medium indirect light, and genuinely tolerant of less — the parlour palm earned its name in Victorian drawing rooms lit by gaslight. Direct sun scorches it.

Humidity and temperature

Above 50% keeps the tips green. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

Ordinary peat-free compost with perlite through it. Palms want moisture-retentive but never waterlogged.

Feeding

A weak balanced feed monthly in spring and summer only. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot every 2 years. Every two or three years, and reluctantly — palm roots resent disturbance. What is sold as one plant is usually a dozen seedlings in a pot; keep them together.

Common problems

  • Brown tips on every frond

    Dry air, hard tap water, or a dry spell. Almost universal indoors.

    Fix: Raise humidity, water with rainwater, and trim the brown off with scissors following the leaf's own shape.

    Brown tips and edges: how to read it on any plant

  • Spider mites

    Dry warm air. Palms are a favourite.

    Fix: Shower the fronds, both sides, and treat weekly for a month.

    Spider mites: how to read it on any plant

  • Whole fronds yellowing from the bottom

    Normal ageing if one at a time; overwatering if several.

    Fix: Cut old fronds off at the base and check the mix is not staying wet.

Propagation

Divide the clump in spring — unusually for a palm, lady palms sucker and divide well.

Toxicity and safety

Non-toxic to cats and dogs.

The ASPCA lists Lady Palm (Rhapis flabelliformus) as non-toxic to cats and dogs (checked 24 September 2026).

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. We don't test plants: what we say about toxicity follows the ASPCA's published plant list as it stood on 24 September 2026, and that list can change. "Non-toxic" means not poisonous — a pet that eats enough of any plant can still vomit, and fertiliser, pesticide or leaf shine on a plant can harm it. It also only helps if the plant is what you think it is. It covers cats and dogs only; ask your vet about rabbits, birds, reptiles or anything else, and a doctor about children. Read our terms.

How often should you water a Lady palm?
