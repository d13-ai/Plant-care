Dragon tail (Epipremnum pinnatum) care

Aroids · easy to keep

Last updated 24 September 2026

Dragon tail (Epipremnum pinnatum) wants anything from bright indirect to a dim corner. Water it every 8 days. Water when the top half of the pot is dry — pothos droops its whole canopy when thirsty and stands back up within a couple of hours of a drink. Pot it in standard potting compost with a handful of perlite. A balanced feed at half strength once a month in spring and summer, and repot once a year. Humidity: indifferent. Ordinary room air is fine. Toxic to cats, dogs and people if chewed — the sap carries insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which burn the mouth and throat.

A close relative of pothos that splits its leaves as it matures, so an established plant looks like a slender monstera. The blue-leaved form, 'Cebu Blue', is the one most people buy — juvenile leaves have a metallic blue sheen that it loses as it climbs and splits.

Watering a Dragon tail

Water every 8 days. Water when the top half of the pot is dry — pothos droops its whole canopy when thirsty and stands back up within a couple of hours of a drink.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Pothos is the plant that made 'tolerates low light' a cliché, and it earns it — but tolerating is not thriving. In a dark spot the leaves come smaller and the variegation fades; in bright indirect light the vines run and the pattern is crisp.

Humidity and temperature

Indifferent. Ordinary room air is fine. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

Pothos is not fussy. Ordinary compost with perlite mixed through for drainage does the job; it will also grow indefinitely in a jar of water, though a plant raised in water sulks if you later pot it into soil.

Feeding

A balanced feed at half strength once a month in spring and summer. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot once a year. Every other spring. If the vines have gone long and bare at the top, cut them back hard at repotting time and push the cuttings into the same pot — that is how a full, bushy pothos is made.

Common problems

  • Yellow leaves scattered through the plant

    Overwatering. Pothos is much more often killed by kindness than by neglect.

    Fix: Let it dry out properly between waterings and make sure the pot actually drains.

    Yellow leaves: how to read it on any plant

  • Variegation disappearing, new leaves plain green

    Not enough light.

    Fix: Move it brighter. Cut back to the last well-marked leaf; all-green growth will otherwise take over, because it grows faster.

    Variegation fading: how to read it on any plant

  • Long stretches of bare vine

    Light coming from one direction and no pruning.

    Fix: Cut the bare vines back to 15 cm; new shoots come from the nodes below the cut.

    Leggy, stretched growth: how to read it on any plant

  • Black soft patches at the base of the stems

    Root rot from standing water.

    Fix: Take healthy cuttings from the top of the vines, root those, and start again — a rotted crown rarely recovers.

Propagation

The easiest cutting in the house. A 10 cm piece with two nodes roots in a glass of water in a fortnight. Change the water weekly.

A cutting taken in PlantParlour keeps its link to the plant it came from, so a propagation years later still traces back to the mother plant — which is the point if you ever sell or trade it.

Toxicity and safety

Toxic to cats, dogs and people if chewed — the sap carries insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which burn the mouth and throat. Keep it off the floor if anything in the house tastes its way through life.

The ASPCA does not list Epipremnum pinnatum itself, but lists a relative, Devils Ivy (Epipremnum aureum), as toxic to cats and dogs (checked 24 September 2026).

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

Named cultivars and variegations

Epipremnum pinnatum is sold under several cultivar names. Care is the same unless noted; variegated forms carry less chlorophyll, so they grow more slowly and want more light than the plain species.
