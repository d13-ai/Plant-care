Pink quill (Tillandsia cyanea) care

Bromeliads & air plants · easy to keep

Last updated 15 October 2026

The pink quill: a grassy rosette with a flat, paddle-shaped pink bract from which small violet flowers emerge one or two at a time over several weeks. Unlike the air plants, it is sold and grown in a pot of bark.

Watering a Pink quill

Water weekly. Water the bark lightly when it is nearly dry, and mist the leaves now and then. Don't leave water standing in the base of the rosette.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Bright indirect light. Bromeliads take more shade than most flowering plants but need brightness to keep their colour.

Humidity and temperature

Above 60%. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

A coarse, fast-draining orchid bark mix in a small pot. It uses its roots for anchoring more than for feeding, and rots in heavy compost.

Feeding

A very weak bromeliad feed in the soaking water once a month. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot every 2 years. Rarely. After flowering the parent slowly fades; pot up the pups once they are a third of its size.

Common problems

  • The parent fading after flowering

    Normal: it flowers once, then finishes.

    Fix: Keep the pups growing; they are the next plants.

  • Brown, crisp leaf tips

    Dry air.

    Fix: Mist it, or raise the humidity.

    Brown tips and edges: how to read it on any plant

  • Rot at the base

    The bark stayed wet.

    Fix: Water less often, and use a coarser mix.

Propagation

Separate the pups that form at the base once they are a third of the parent's size, with some roots attached.

A cutting taken in PlantParlour keeps its link to the plant it came from, so a propagation years later still traces back to the mother plant — which is the point if you ever sell or trade it.

Toxicity and safety

Not on the ASPCA's list of toxic or non-toxic plants, so we don't call it pet-safe: not being listed is not the same as being tested. Keep it out of reach of a pet that chews.

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. We don't test plants: what we say about toxicity follows the ASPCA's published plant list as it stood on 15 October 2026, and that list can change. "Non-toxic" means not poisonous — a pet that eats enough of any plant can still vomit, and fertiliser, pesticide or leaf shine on a plant can harm it. It also only helps if the plant is what you think it is. It covers cats and dogs only; ask your vet about rabbits, birds, reptiles or anything else, and a doctor about children. Read our terms.

Questions people ask

How often should you water a Pink quill?

Water weekly as a starting point — that is the cadence PlantParlour sets for Tillandsia cyanea. Water the bark lightly when it is nearly dry, and mist the leaves now and then. Don't leave water standing in the base of the rosette. Light, pot size and the time of year all move that number, so treat it as a reminder to go and check rather than a rule.
