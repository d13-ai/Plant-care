Prince of Orange (Philodendron 'Prince of Orange') care

Aroids · easy to keep

Prince of Orange (Philodendron 'Prince of Orange') wants bright indirect light; tolerates a shadier spot than most. Water it weekly. Water when the top third of the pot is dry. Philodendrons wilt visibly when genuinely thirsty and recover within hours, which makes them easy to read. Pot it in loose, airy aroid mix with plenty of bark and perlite. Balanced liquid feed at half strength every third watering through the growing season, and repot once a year. Humidity: fine at room humidity, faster growth above 60%. Toxic to cats, dogs and people if chewed — the sap carries insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which burn the mouth and throat.

A self-heading philodendron — it forms a rosette rather than climbing — whose new leaves emerge bright copper-orange and age through amber to green, so an established plant shows the whole gradient at once. Compact enough for a shelf and one of the easier collector philodendrons.

Watering a Prince of Orange

Water weekly. Water when the top third of the pot is dry. Philodendrons wilt visibly when genuinely thirsty and recover within hours, which makes them easy to read.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Bright indirect light suits them, and most philodendrons cope with genuinely dim corners better than a monstera will — they just grow slower and stretch. Variegated forms are the exception: white and pink tissue cannot photosynthesise, so those plants need more light than their green siblings, not less.

Humidity and temperature

Fine at room humidity, faster growth above 60%. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

Almost all of them climb or creep rather than sit in deep soil, so the roots want air. Half potting compost, half bark and perlite is the standard recipe. Crawlers like gloriosum want a shallow wide pot rather than a deep one — the rhizome runs along the surface.

Feeding

Balanced liquid feed at half strength every third watering through the growing season. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot once a year. Every spring or two, one size up. A philodendron that has filled its pot pushes roots out of the drainage holes and dries out within a day or two of watering — that is the signal.

Common problems

  • Long bare stems with leaves far apart

    Too little light. The plant is reaching.

    Fix: Move it brighter and cut the leggy growth back — it will branch from the nodes below the cut.

    Leggy, stretched growth: how to read it on any plant

  • Yellow lower leaves

    Usually overwatering; occasionally just age, one leaf at a time.

    Fix: If several go at once, check the roots. Brown and soft means rot; trim them and repot into fresh airy mix.

    Yellow leaves: how to read it on any plant

  • Variegation fading back to plain green

    Not enough light for the plant to afford the white tissue.

    Fix: Give it more light, and prune back to a node that still shows variegation — the plant grows on from the last variegated point.

    Variegation fading: how to read it on any plant

  • Brown tips on new leaves that were fine in the old pot

    Low humidity while the leaf was still unfurling.

    Fix: Group plants together or run a humidifier while new leaves open; the damage is cosmetic and permanent on that leaf.

    Brown tips and edges: how to read it on any plant

Propagation

Stem cuttings root readily: take a section with at least one node and a leaf, and put it in water, perlite or damp sphagnum. Crawling species are divided instead, by cutting the rhizome between two growth points.

A cutting taken in PlantParlour keeps its link to the plant it came from, so a propagation years later still traces back to the mother plant — which is the point if you ever sell or trade it.

Toxicity and safety

Toxic to cats, dogs and people if chewed — the sap carries insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which burn the mouth and throat. Keep it off the floor if anything in the house tastes its way through life.

The ASPCA lists Philodendron Pertusum (Philodendron spp) as toxic to cats and dogs (checked 24 September 2026).

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. "Non-toxic" means not poisonous — a pet that eats enough of any plant can still vomit, and fertiliser, pesticide or leaf shine on a plant can harm it.
