Anthurium crystallinum care

Aroids · needs attention to keep

Last updated 24 September 2026

Anthurium crystallinum wants bright indirect light, never direct sun. Water it weekly. Water when the top of the mix is dry to the touch, and let it drain completely. In a bark mix that is often twice a week; in soil it would be a death sentence. Pot it in very chunky — mostly bark and perlite, barely any soil. A weak orchid feed every second watering while growing, and repot once a year. Humidity: above 70%. Toxic to cats, dogs and people if chewed — the sap carries insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which burn the mouth and throat.

The other great velvet anthurium: thinner and more elongated than clarinervium, with a crystalline shimmer across the leaf and silver-white veining. New leaves emerge coppery pink and harden off dark green. It is more humidity-dependent than clarinervium and scars easily if the air is dry while a leaf is opening.

Watering an Anthurium crystallinum

Water weekly. Water when the top of the mix is dry to the touch, and let it drain completely. In a bark mix that is often twice a week; in soil it would be a death sentence.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Bright indirect light. The flowering kinds need it to keep producing spathes; the velvet-leaved ones scorch easily and want the same brightness filtered through a sheer curtain.

Humidity and temperature

Above 70%. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

Anthuriums are epiphytes. The mix should look wrong: mostly orchid bark and perlite with a little coir, loose enough that water pours straight through. Standard compost suffocates them within a season.

Feeding

A weak orchid feed every second watering while growing. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot once a year. Every year or two, into a pot barely larger than the root ball. Bury the stem slightly deeper each time — anthuriums creep upward and go bald at the base otherwise.

Common problems

  • Brown crispy leaf edges

    Air too dry, or tap water minerals building up.

    Fix: Raise humidity above 60% and water with rain or filtered water if your tap is hard.

  • No flowers for months

    Not enough light, or not enough phosphorus.

    Fix: Move it brighter and switch to a feed with a higher middle number through spring and summer.

  • Yellowing leaves with a soft brown base

    Rot, almost always from a mix that holds water.

    Fix: Repot into bark and perlite, cutting away any root that is brown and mushy.

  • A bald woody stem with leaves only at the top

    Normal upward creep.

    Fix: Repot deeper, or cut the top off below a set of aerial roots and re-root it.

Propagation

Divide at the base where a plant has made side shoots with their own roots. Velvet species also produce seed from a pollinated spadix, but division is the practical route.

Toxicity and safety

Toxic to cats, dogs and people if chewed — the sap carries insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which burn the mouth and throat. Keep it off the floor if anything in the house tastes its way through life.

The ASPCA does not list Anthurium crystallinum itself, but lists a relative, Flamingo Flower (Anthurium scherzeranum), as toxic to cats and dogs (checked 24 September 2026).

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.
