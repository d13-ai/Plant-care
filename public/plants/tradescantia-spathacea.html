Moses-in-the-cradle (Tradescantia spathacea) care

Vines & trailers · easy to keep

Last updated 15 October 2026

Moses-in-the-cradle (Tradescantia spathacea) wants bright indirect light; the colours need it. Water it weekly. Let the top few centimetres dry between waterings. The thick leaves store water, and it copes with drying out better than the trailing tradescantias. Pot it in standard compost with perlite. A balanced feed at half strength monthly in spring and summer, and repot once a year. Humidity: room humidity is fine. Mildly toxic to cats and dogs; the sap can also cause a skin rash in people who handle it a lot.

Moses-in-the-cradle, or oyster plant: stiff rosettes of sword-shaped leaves, green or striped on top and purple underneath, with small white flowers tucked into boat-shaped bracts at the base — the 'cradle'. Unlike its trailing relatives it grows upright, in clumps.

Watering a Moses-in-the-cradle

Water weekly. Let the top few centimetres dry between waterings. The thick leaves store water, and it copes with drying out better than the trailing tradescantias.

That cadence is the one PlantParlour sets for this species when you name it, and it is deliberately on the cautious side — a plant that asks a day early is safer than one that asks a day late. Log each watering and the reminder moves with the plant rather than with the calendar.

Light

Bright indirect light, with an hour of direct sun welcome. The purple, pink and cream stripes wash out to plain green in a dim spot faster than almost any other houseplant.

Humidity and temperature

Room humidity is fine. Central heating in winter is what turns a comfortable room into a dry one, and it is the usual reason an established plant suddenly browns at the edges or picks up spider mites.

Soil and potting

Ordinary potting compost with perlite. Tradescantias are unfussy and grow at a speed that makes the mix almost irrelevant.

Feeding

A balanced feed at half strength monthly in spring and summer. Stop feeding in autumn and start again when you see new growth in spring — feeding a plant that isn't growing just builds up salts in the pot.

Repotting

Repot once a year. Every year or two in spring, once the clump has filled the pot. Divide it then.

Common problems

  • Stripes fading to green

    Not enough light.

    Fix: Move it brighter; it takes a little direct sun.

  • Rot at the base

    The compost stayed wet.

    Fix: Let it dry between waterings, and make sure the pot drains.

  • Brown leaf tips

    Dry air, or salts from tap water.

    Fix: Raise the humidity and flush the pot through now and then.

Propagation

Separate the offsets that form around the base and pot them up.

A cutting taken in PlantParlour keeps its link to the plant it came from, so a propagation years later still traces back to the mother plant — which is the point if you ever sell or trade it.

Toxicity and safety

Mildly toxic to cats and dogs; the sap can also cause a skin rash in people who handle it a lot.

The ASPCA does not list Tradescantia spathacea itself, but lists a relative, Inch Plant (Tradescantia flumeninsis), as toxic to cats and dogs (checked 15 October 2026).

If your pet has eaten a plant, call your vet, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, or the Pet Poison Helpline on (855) 764-7661. Both are open around the clock and may charge a consultation fee. Take the plant or its label with you.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. We don't test plants: what we say about toxicity follows the ASPCA's published plant list as it stood on 15 October 2026, and that list can change. "Non-toxic" means not poisonous — a pet that eats enough of any plant can still vomit, and fertiliser, pesticide or leaf shine on a plant can harm it. It also only helps if the plant is what you think it is. It covers cats and dogs only; ask your vet about rabbits, birds, reptiles or anything else, and a doctor about children. Read our terms.
